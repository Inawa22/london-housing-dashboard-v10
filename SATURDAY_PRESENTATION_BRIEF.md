# Saturday client presentation — testing London's housing supply hypothesis

**Working research question:** Does London's housing delivery keep pace with its housing need, and could a shortfall help explain high costs and unmet housing need?

**Format:** Four to six minutes, using the new **🔎 · Test the hypothesis** start page. It is a working analytical update, not a final verdict or causal model.

## Suggested verbal introduction (40 seconds)

> I've reorganised the first three objectives around one testable question: is London adding enough homes to meet housing need? I distinguish *how many homes already exist*, *how many are being added each year*, and *how many new homes are assessed as needed*. Those are not interchangeable. I'll show what we know, where the evidence is incomplete, and what this implies for our eventual scenario modelling.

## Slide / dashboard view 1 — Does delivery meet assessed need? (60–75 seconds)

1. Click **01 · Test the supply gap**.
2. Show **32,680 net additional dwellings** in London in financial year 2024–25 (MHCLG accredited official statistics).
3. Show the government's current **approximately 88,000 homes a year** requirement under its *standard method* (a planning estimate, not a direct count of waiting households).
4. Under these two measures, observed 2024–25 additions equate to approximately **37.1% of annual modelled need**, a difference of **55,320 homes in that one-year comparison**.
5. Explain carefully: this does **not** prove that the shortage is 55,320 households, or that the gap persisted for every previous year. Switching the benchmark to the **2021 London Plan target (~52,000)** or the **2017 GLA SHMA (~66,000)** gives different results: those are *different methods and vintages*, not interchangeable demand measurements.

**Possible one-sentence finding:** “The most defensible first result is a shortfall in annual housing *delivery relative to a stated planning requirement*, not yet proof of a persistently undersupplied housing market.”

## View 2 — Total supply grew despite the gap (45 seconds)

The Table 100 data show London's dwelling stock increasing from **3,387,821** in 2012 to **3,854,594** in 2025 (**13.8%** growth). This tells us London has added stock. It does not tell us whether these additions met underlying need; existing stock includes occupied and vacant homes. The annual stock change in the latest year is **32,678** (calculated from two rounded/estimated stock totals), which closely matches the separately published net additions figure of **32,680**.

**Why this matters:** A growing stock is perfectly compatible with a shortfall in *annual* delivery relative to an estimated requirement.

## View 3 — Are there signs of pressure? (45 seconds)

The MHCLG data record **75,600 households** in temporary accommodation in London at 2025 Q4, up from **73,320** in Q1, but down slightly from 75,800 in Q3. The separate ONS rental model estimates London-wide average private rent at **£2,332 per month** in August 2026. The rental figure is nominal and not an income-based affordability measure. We must *not* sum households in temporary accommodation, prevention/relief duties and CHAIN people seen rough sleeping, because they describe overlapping needs and different units.

**Avoid:** “The annual building gap caused 75,600 homeless households.” Our dataset does not support that causal assertion.

## View 4 — The borough puzzle (60 seconds)

Newham is a thought-provoking case: the number of existing dwellings rose **30.3%** between 2012 and 2025, while its 2025 Q4 temporary-accommodation rate was **59.0 households per 1,000 households**, the highest reported borough rate that quarter. This is a coexistence pattern, not proof that supply expansion had no benefit. Without comparing to a valid counterfactual, we cannot know what Newham's need would have been if those homes had *not* been built.

**Good client question:** Is housing need predominantly a total-unit shortage, or a shortage of suitable, affordable housing of specific tenures and locations?

## Close — The next investigation (30–45 seconds)

> My current conclusion is that annual delivery is below the *selected* modelled requirement in the latest year, and several independent data series show continuing pressure. I cannot yet claim that *market demand consistently exceeded supply* throughout the period, or that adding a specified number of homes would reduce rents by a specified percentage. Next, I would compile a consistent multiyear housing-need versus delivery series, add overcrowding and social housing register data by borough, and study the timing and income/tenure mix before creating the Objective 4 scenario model.

## Definitions client explicitly requested

- **Dwelling stock / existing capacity:** Number of self-contained residential dwellings on a date, including vacant properties. A stock.
- **Annual housing delivery / net additions:** New builds plus net conversions/changes of use, minus losses/demolitions, across a financial year. A flow.
- **Demand:** Willingness and ability to occupy/pay for homes at varying prices; not directly observed in the current dashboard.
- **Planning housing need:** Annual requirement produced by an explicitly defined modelling/policy framework. Not interchangeable with effective demand.
- **Unmet housing need:** Households/people without suitable housing, only partly observed by statutory homelessness, temporary accommodation, CHAIN, overcrowding and housing registers.
- **Cost:** Observed/modelled rents and purchase prices. **Affordability** additionally requires household income and finances.

## Questions to ask the client at the end

1. Are you most interested in the **total annual delivery gap**, or specifically the **affordable/social-housing delivery gap**?
2. Should the eventual Objective 4 model target **London-wide rents**, **borough-level rents**, or the **number of households in temporary accommodation**? The datasets and causal assumptions differ.
3. Do you want the hypothesis tested over **the last decade** (requiring aligned need-series history) or the most recent full year first?

## Data provenance and reliability

| Indicator | Publisher, data & period | Reliability / caution |
|---|---|---|
| 32,680 net additions | MHCLG, 2024–25 net supply release | Accredited official statistics; London borough data provisional. Net additions include more than new builds. |
| ~88,000 needed annually | Government standard method, as summarised by House of Commons Library | A planning formula based on existing stock and affordability signals, not observed applications or a pure demographic forecast; rounded figure. |
| ~52,000 London Plan target | London Plan 2021, cited by London Assembly | Historic *planning target*, not directly equivalent to modelled need. |
| ~66,000 GLA SHMA need | GLA 2017 assessment, cited by London Assembly | Historic need estimate, built using older population/projection methods. |
| Existing dwellings 2012–25 | MHCLG Table 100 | 2022–25 provisional; rounded/modelled components and possible historical revisions. |
